\section{Overview}

This report compares four parameter-estimation methods across two point-process
families, each trained for 3 seeds (9371--9373) with the same train/val/test split
per seed within a process, so every method sees identical data:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{vihrs} --- classical baseline: a 1-D CNN over the centred Ripley
    $L(r)-r$ function plus the point count $n(x)$, reproducing Vihrs (2022).
  \item \textbf{pi\_multik (k=5,10,15)} --- this repo's multi-scale persistence-image
    CNN: a shared CoordConv encoder applied independently to DTM-filtration
    persistence images at three density scales ($k=5,10,15$), late-fused before an
    MLP head.
  \item \textbf{mph\_pi} --- a CNN over a genuinely \emph{multiparameter} persistent
    homology image: a single joint (density, radius) bifiltration signed measure,
    rendered as one $(H_0,H_1)$ image pair. Architecturally identical to
    \texttt{pi\_multik}'s single-scale branch (CoordConv encoder + MLP head) ---
    the only structural difference is that scale is already folded into the second
    filtration axis, so there is no $k$-axis to fuse across.
  \item \textbf{mph\_fusion} --- late fusion of \texttt{vihrs}'s $L(r)-r$ branch and
    \texttt{mph\_pi}'s CoordConv branch, motivated by the two methods specializing
    in different targets (Section~\ref{sec:nested-thomas}).
\end{itemize}

All targets are trained in \emph{standardized log space}: each target is
log-transformed, then $z$-scored using the mean/std fit on that seed's training
split; the reported loss is always mean squared error (MSE) on that standardized
scale. Section~\ref{sec:units} explains exactly what a given loss value means in
the original, physical units of each parameter.

\section{Reading the loss numbers}
\label{sec:units}

Every loss in this report is a standardized MSE, not a raw squared error in
physical units --- so ``0.03'' does not mean the same thing for every target, and
does not mean anything at all without translation. This section makes that
translation concrete.

\subsection{Why the scale is arbitrary without context}

Each target $y$ (e.g.\ \texttt{parent\_intensity}) is transformed before training
as $z = \bigl(\log y - \mu_{\log}\bigr)/\sigma_{\log}$, where $\mu_{\log}$ and
$\sigma_{\log}$ are the mean and standard deviation of $\log y$ on that seed's
\emph{training} split. The model is scored by $\mathrm{MSE} =
\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat z - z)^2\bigr]$ on the test split. Two properties fall out of
this construction:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The ``no-skill'' baseline is $\approx 1$.} A model that always
    predicts the training mean ($\hat z = 0$) scores $\mathrm{MSE} \approx
    \mathrm{Var}(z) \approx 1$ (by construction, on data drawn like the training
    set). So loss values are naturally read against 1.0, not against 0: a loss of
    0.5 means the model is explaining about half the variance in log-space, a loss
    of 0.03 means it is explaining almost all of it.
  \item \textbf{$R^2 \approx 1 - \mathrm{loss}$.} This is the standard
    variance-explained interpretation, directly usable without further
    transformation.
  \item \textbf{The same loss value means a different real-world error for
    different targets}, because $\sigma_{\log}$ (how spread out that target is in
    log-space to begin with) differs by target. A target that already spans a
    narrow log-range needs a much smaller absolute log-error to reach a given
    standardized loss than a target that spans a wide one.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From standardized loss to a physical error factor}

Undoing both transforms (z-score, then log) turns a standardized RMSE into a
\emph{multiplicative} error factor in the original units: if
$\mathrm{RMSE}_z = \sqrt{\mathrm{loss}}$, then a ``typical'' prediction sits within
a factor of $\exp\!\bigl(\mathrm{RMSE}_z \cdot \sigma_{\log}\bigr)$ of the true
value. Tables~\ref{tab:loss-units-nested-thomas} and \ref{tab:loss-units-thomas} apply this to every target actually used in
this report, at a range of representative loss values.

\input{mph_comparison_report_loss_units.tex}

\textbf{Worked example.} A loss of $0.03$ on \texttt{cluster\_scale}
(nested Thomas, $\sigma_{\log}=0.655$) corresponds to $R^2=0.97$ and a typical
prediction within about $\pm12\%$ of the true value; the same loss of $0.03$ on
\texttt{meta\_offspring} ($\sigma_{\log}=0.409$) --- a target with less inherent
log-spread --- corresponds to the same $R^2$ but a tighter $\pm7\%$ typical error.
The take-away: compare loss numbers \emph{within} a target/process across methods
freely, but don't compare raw loss values \emph{across} targets without consulting
Tables~\ref{tab:loss-units-nested-thomas}/\ref{tab:loss-units-thomas} first.

\section{Nested Thomas process}
\label{sec:nested-thomas}

Nested Thomas is a two-level cluster process (meta-clusters of sub-clusters),
with five targets: \texttt{parent\_intensity}, \texttt{meta\_offspring},
\texttt{meta\_cluster\_scale}, \texttt{mean\_offspring}, \texttt{cluster\_scale}.
8000 clouds (7000 train/val/test, 1000 held-out adversarial), $\sigma_{\log}$ per
target in Table~\ref{tab:loss-units-nested-thomas}.

\textbf{Finding: no single method dominates every target, and the strongest
overall performer is a close contest between two very different architectures.}
Overall (all-target) test loss ranks
\texttt{pi\_multik} (0.1927) $\approx$ \texttt{mph\_fusion} (0.1965) $<$
\texttt{mph\_pi} (0.2095) $<$ \texttt{vihrs} (0.2171) --- but that ordering
does not hold target-by-target. \texttt{mph\_pi} (single joint bifiltration
image) is clearly better than \texttt{vihrs} at the intensity/count targets
(\texttt{parent\_intensity}, \texttt{meta\_offspring}); \texttt{vihrs}'s
$L(r)-r$ is clearly better at \texttt{cluster\_scale} --- consistent with what
each feature actually encodes: $L(r)-r$ is built to detect a characteristic
clustering \emph{radius} directly, while the DTM-bifiltration image is
fundamentally a density/topology signal. \texttt{mph\_fusion} (late-fusing
those two) wins \texttt{parent\_intensity} and \texttt{meta\_cluster\_scale}
outright and beats both of its parent methods on overall test loss in all 3/3
seeds. \texttt{pi\_multik} (three single-parameter DTM scales, $k=5,10,15$,
fused) takes a different route to a similarly strong result, winning
\texttt{meta\_offspring} and \texttt{mean\_offspring} outright --- sweeping
several fixed density scales evidently captures much of what the joint
bifiltration captures, at least on this process. \texttt{vihrs} alone wins
only \texttt{cluster\_scale}, but wins it clearly (0.028 vs.\ 0.029--0.043 for
everything else), so it is not dominated either. The practical takeaway: the
choice of TDA representation trades off \emph{which} parameters it resolves
best, not just \emph{how well} on average, and fusion (of either kind) is the
more robust choice when no single target dominates the estimation task.

\section{Thomas process}
\label{sec:thomas}

Plain Thomas is a single-level cluster process, with three targets:
\texttt{parent\_intensity}, \texttt{mean\_offspring}, \texttt{cluster\_scale}.
Same 8000-cloud (7000+1000) split convention as nested Thomas.
\texttt{pi\_multik} for this process was trained on HPC rather than locally
(Section~\ref{sec:comparability}); everything else matches the nested-Thomas
protocol exactly, same 3 seeds.

\textbf{Finding: a cleaner win for fusion here than on nested Thomas.} Overall
test loss ranks \texttt{mph\_fusion} (0.1131) $<$ \texttt{vihrs} (0.1154) $<$

\texttt{pi\_multik} does not lead here --- with only one clustering scale to
resolve (rather than two nested ones), sweeping three DTM $k$ values adds less
on top of a single joint bifiltration or a direct radius estimate.
\texttt{mph\_fusion} wins \texttt{parent\_intensity} and \texttt{mean\_offspring}
outright; \texttt{vihrs} alone still wins \texttt{cluster\_scale} clearly (0.029
vs.\ 0.040 for the DTM-based methods) --- the same pattern as nested Thomas:
$L(r)-r$ remains the sharpest tool specifically for the spatial-scale target,
and fusing it in rather than discarding it is what wins overall.

\section{Notes on comparability}
\label{sec:comparability}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Training recipes differ.} \texttt{vihrs} and \texttt{pi\_multik}
    use their own bespoke training loops (Adam/AdamW with early stopping on
    validation loss); \texttt{mph\_pi}/\texttt{mph\_fusion} use this repo's shared
    single-source training runner (plain Adam, fixed weight decay, no early
    stopping --- always trains the full epoch budget). All four reload the
    best-validation-loss checkpoint before final test/adversarial evaluation, so
    final numbers are comparable despite the different stopping rules; the
    difference only costs \texttt{mph\_pi}/\texttt{mph\_fusion} extra wall-clock
    time; it does not advantage either side numerically.
  \item \textbf{Data provenance.} All numbers here come from data generated (or
    regenerated) after this report's underlying pipeline changes; two data issues
    were found and fixed while producing these results: \texttt{nested\_thomas}'s
    $k=5$ diagrams had never finished computing, and \texttt{thomas}'s point
    clouds had been lost (the $k=5/10/15$ diagrams on disk no longer matched any
    reproducible cloud set) and were regenerated from the process's seeded design.
  \item \textbf{Compute location.} \texttt{thomas}'s \texttt{pi\_multik} (3
    seeds) was trained on HPC and its results downloaded rather than trained
    locally, after the local diagram-computation step for this method proved too
    memory-heavy for reliable local execution (see the repo history around
    \texttt{scripts/featurize.py}'s per-cloud persistent-homology computation).
    Every other number in this report --- including \texttt{thomas}'s
    \texttt{vihrs}/\texttt{mph\_pi}/\texttt{mph\_fusion} runs, which were
    \emph{also} re-obtained from that same HPC download after a local crash lost
    the original local copies --- was validated (clean unpickle, correct label
    set, expected record counts) before being incorporated here, but was not
    independently re-run in both places to confirm byte-identical reproduction.
  \item \textbf{Adversarial loss} is evaluated on a held-out population generated
    with a disjoint seed offset, never seen during training or model selection.
\end{itemize}
