\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% #############################################################################

The study of spatial point processes finds applications in astronomy, cell
biology, epidemiology, etc \citep{moller2004statistical}. Many natural phenomena
-- such as galaxy clustering -- are modelled using SPPs, so one can hope to
leverage an understanding of these processes to obtain novel insights. The
parameters of spatial point processes are of particular interest because they
are interpretable scientific quantities. For example, when Neyman-Scott
processes are used to model galaxy clustering the parent intensity $\kappa$
corresponds to the number density of haloes hosting galaxies, the offspring
intensity $\mu$ is the mean occupancy of a halo, and the cluster scale $\sigma$
is the spatial extent of the galaxy distribution within a halo REF.

Existing estimation methods typically rely on closed-form expressions for
summary statistics such as the $K$ and $L$ functions. Minimum contrast
estimation fits parameters by numerically minimizing a discrepancy between the
empirical and theoretical curve, most commonly for $K$ or $g$
\citep{diggle2003spatial}. Palm likelihood instead builds a composite likelihood
from the process's Palm intensity, developed specifically for Neyman-Scott
processes \citep{tanaka2008parameter}.

These methods share three limitations. First, they rely on closed-form summary statistics, which may not exist (the LGCP-Strauss process of
\citet{vihrs2022neural} has no tractable density) or may exist but fail the
regularity conditions the estimator's theory needs (the two-step estimator of
\citet{waagepetersen2009twostep} requires $K_\psi$ to be twice continuously
differentiable in $\psi$, which excludes the Mat\'ern cluster process). Second, they are sensitive to hyperparameters chosen per dataset by rule of thumb, with no held-out quantity indicating whether the choice was good: minimum contrast depends on $(r_{max}, q, p)$, and \citet{waagepetersen2009twostep} report that Thomas estimates of $\kappa$ and $\omega$ are ``quite sensitive'' to $r_{max}$ and $q$, being strongly negatively correlated so that the product $\hat\kappa\hat\omega$ is stable while the individual estimates are not. Finally,
they destabilise as the process approaches complete spatial randomness: writing $\lambda=\kappa\mu$ for the expected number of points per unit area and $\nu = \sigma\sqrt{\kappa}$ for the ratio of cluster radius to mean parent spacing, once $\nu \gtrsim 1$ neighbouring clusters overlap and many
$(\kappa,\mu)$ pairs with the same product produce nearly the same observable
pattern. The theoretical $K$-function makes this explicit -- for a Thomas
process $K(t) = \pi t^2 + (1-e^{-t^2/4\sigma^2})/\kappa$, so as $\sigma$ grows at fixed $t$ the second term vanishes and $K$ tends to the Poisson $K$
regardless of $\kappa$ -- and the contrast surface is close to flat along this
trade-off, so estimates become sensitive to the starting point.

This prompted me to consider whether persistence-based features could be used in
this context, and whether they have advantages over these classical methods:
they provide a multi-scale, process-agnostic descriptor of a point cloud that
requires neither a closed form nor a choice of summary statistic.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Problem setup}
\label{ssec:setup}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let $X$ be a Neyman-Scott process on $\R^d$ with unknown parameter vector
$\theta \in \R^{n_{params}}$. For example, the homogeneous Thomas process has
$\theta = (\kappa, \mu, \sigma)$ corresponding to parent intensity, offspring
intensity and cluster scale, respectively. We observe a single realization of
$X$ on a bounded window $W \subset \R^d$, giving a point cloud
$\pc = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subset \R^d$ with $n = |X \cap W|$. The objective is
to produce an estimate $\hat\theta = \hat\theta(\pc)$ of $\theta$ from $\pc$
alone. We stress \emph{single realization}: $\theta$ is not recovered from
repeated draws of $X$. This is the setting a practitioner faces: one observed
pattern and one chance to estimate the generating parameters.

The model is trained on a set of synthetic clouds
$\{\pc_i, \theta_i\}_{i=1}^{N_{train}}$ by backpropagating the RMS loss
    \[
    \mathcal{L}(\hat\theta_i, \theta_i) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n_{params}}(\hat\theta^j_i - \theta^j_i)^2}.
    \]
The scale of each parameter varies; therefore -- if not normalized -- the
large-scale parameters will dominate this loss. To address this, we train in
log-normalized parameter space, mapping
$\theta_i \mapsto (\log(\theta_i) - \mu_{\log\theta})/\sigma_{\log\theta}$,
where -- abusing notation -- $\mu_{\log\theta}, \sigma_{\log\theta}$ denote the
mean and standard deviation of the logged parameters over the training set. All
losses reported below are in this space, so that $\mathcal{L}\approx 1$
corresponds to chance level.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% #############################################################################

\citet{vihrs2022neural} first proposed the use of deep learning for parameter
estimation. \citet{yip2025learning} employ a similar architecture with
persistence images as the input feature, mapping $H_0$--$H_2$ images of an
$\alpha\mathrm{DTM}\ell$ filtration of a halo catalogue to
$(\Omega_m,\sigma_8)$; their targets are cosmological parameters rather than
those of the underlying point process, and they do not systematically compare
topological summaries. Which summary to use is an important and ongoing question in this investigation, since
persistence diagrams do not live in a Hilbert space and cannot be fed directly
to a machine learning model: \citet{adams2017persistence} propose persistence
images, \citet{umeda2017time} Betti curves, \citet{bubenik2015landscapes}
persistence landscapes and \citet{chazal2014silhouette} silhouettes, while
\citet{reininghaus2015kernel} avoid vectorization altogether with a kernel on
diagrams.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Neyman-Scott processes}
\label{ssec:spp}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A spatial point process on $W \subseteq \R^d$ is characterised by its intensity
function $\rho(u)$, which satisfies $\E[N(W)] = \int_W \rho(u)\,du$ for
$N(W) = |W \cap X|$; when $\rho$ is constant the process is homogeneous, the
canonical example being the Poisson process. A Neyman-Scott process is a
clustered process built on top of a Poisson; the processes used throughout this work follow the standard construction: expand the window $W$ by an edge buffer to give $W_{exp}$, so that
clusters with parents outside $W$ are not truncated; sample a homogeneous
Poisson \emph{parent} process of intensity $\kappa$ on $W_{exp}$; for each
parent $p$ draw an offspring count $n_p \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)$; place each
offspring at $p + \epsilon$ with displacement $\epsilon$ drawn from a kernel
$k$; finally discard the parents and retain the offspring falling in $W$.

Every Neyman-Scott process therefore has the parent and offspring intensities
$(\kappa,\mu)$ as parameters, and the choice of kernel supplies the rest: for
the \emph{Thomas} process $k = \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I_d)$, giving
$\theta = (\kappa,\mu,\sigma)$; for the Mat\'ern cluster process $k$ is uniform
on a ball of radius $R$; and for the \emph{nested Thomas} process the parent
process at the second step is itself a Thomas process rather than a Poisson one,
producing clusters of clusters and a five-parameter target.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Persistent homology}
\label{ssec:persistence}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Persistent homology (PH)  tracks a growing family of simplicial complexes built on a point cloud. A
\emph{filtration function} $f$ assigns to each subset of points a scale at
which it appears in the resulting filtration $\filt_r$, subject to a monotonicity condition, and the sublevel sets
$\filt_r = \{\sigma : f(\sigma)\le r\}$ form a nested family of complexes
indexed by $r$. The canonical choice is Vietoris-Rips, where a simplex enters at
its diameter; the DTM$_k$ filtration \citep{anai2020dtm} instead uses each
point's root-mean-square distance to its $k$ nearest neighbours, so a simplex
enters according to how densely its neighbourhood is occupied rather than how
close its points happen to be. This preserves more information about the local density of the point cloud, which is particularly useful in this context, as the generating parameters of a point process directly affect this density.

As $r$ grows, homological features -- connected components ($H_0$) and loops
($H_1$) -- are \emph{born} and later \emph{die}
by merging into older features. Each is recorded as a pair $(b,d)$ of birth and death scales, and the multiset of all such pairs in dimension $h$ is the
\emph{persistence diagram} $D_h$; the lifetime $d-b$ is often taken as a measure of a feature's robustness, so
points near the diagonal are usually read as noise. Applied to a point pattern,
$H_0$ records the order and scales at which clusters merge and $H_1$ the gaps
and voids the clustering leaves behind, neither requiring a closed form or an assumption about which family generated the pattern. As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:background}, persistence diagrams are multisets rather than vectors, so they must be \emph{vectorized}
before being used for machine learning; the
persistence image \citep{adams2017persistence} maps to birth-persistence
coordinates $(b, d-b)$, places a weighted Gaussian bump at each point, and
integrates the resulting surface over a $64\times64$ pixel grid.

The principal difference between features based on persistence summaries and those based on classical ... Radially averaged statistics such as $g$ and $K$ collapse a point cloud to a
real-valued curve indexed by scale $r$, and are turned into features by
evaluating over $[r_{min}, r_{max}]$ and subsampling to obtain a vector in
$\R^n$; this discards multi-scale connectivity and void structure, i.e.\
\emph{how} the density is distributed across space.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Synthetic cloud generation}
\label{ssec:simulator}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Since the estimator is trained rather than derived, the simulator plays the role
that a closed-form summary statistic plays in a classical method: it defines
what the model is asked to learn. Training data are pairs $(\pc_i, \theta_i)$
where $\theta_i$ is drawn from a \emph{design distribution} over parameter space
and $\pc_i$ is a single realization of the corresponding process, generated by
the construction of Section~\ref{ssec:spp}. The design distribution therefore
acts as a prior, and the qualitative regimes it covers determine where the
trained model is usable.

After adopting this naive approach at first, I found that sampling each parameter uniformly and independently from some interval is inadequate, because the regimes of interest occupy curved subsets of
the parameter grid and most of the mass landed on configurations that were
near-Poisson or degenerate. 

To address this, I moved to sampling in reparameterized coordinates that allow for more direct control of these regimes: the expected intensity
$\lambda = \kappa\mu$, the parent intensity $\kappa$, and the dimensionless
overlap index $\nu = r\sqrt{\kappa}$, where $r$ is the cluster scale of the
family in question ($r=\sigma$ for Thomas, $r=\sqrt{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}$ for nested Thomas, with the extra coordinate $\rho = \sigma_2/\sigma_1$). Since $\nu$ measures cluster radius against mean inter-parent spacing, $\nu \ll 1$ gives well-separated clusters and $\nu \gtrsim 1$ clusters that merge. Draws are log-uniform, since the parameters span orders of magnitude, and are constrained so that $\lambda \in [150,800]$ (persistence computation scales badly in the number of points), $\kappa \in [15,120]$ (enough clusters per window to be informative, few enough to remain resolvable), and $\nu \in [0.10,0.90]$. The upper limit on $\nu$ deliberately retains the near-Poisson regime in which classical estimators are least stable, rather than restricting to well-separated clusters. Rejection acts on the design and not on realizations, so it does not distort the conditional law of $\pc$ given $\theta$.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Model: ParamNet}
\label{ssec:method}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let $\pc$ denote a point cloud, $v$ its vectorized persistence summary, and
$e \in \R^{E + 1}$ the resulting feature vector, where $E$ denotes the encoder's
embedding dimension and the $+1$ is the appended $\log N(\pc)$. ParamNet
follows the pipeline
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pipeline}
    \pc \xrightarrow{\;\text{Vectorization}\;} v
    \xrightarrow{\;\text{Encoder}\;} e
    \xrightarrow{\;\text{FCNN}\;} \hat\theta.
    \end{equation}
The pipeline shape follows the neural-estimator patterns used in
\citet{vihrs2022neural} and \citet{yip2025learning}; what I have added is the
application to a more challenging process (nested Thomas), an explicit
evaluation of the design choices at each stage, and a comparison of TDA-based
vectorizations, on which there has been no formalized discussion in this
setting.

\paragraph{Filtration.} My starting point was the canonical Rips filtration,
but it is built from inter-point distances alone: it is sensitive to the
sparsest connection between two regions and does not register how densely those
regions are occupied. Since the parameters of a point process act on local
density, DTM$_k$ seemed the richer choice, and the experiments
confirmed this -- Rips is the worst filtration arm on both processes, by a wide margin on nested Thomas (Table~\ref{tab:branches}). Within DTM, error grows monotonically with $k$, consistent with a finer $k$-nearest neighbourhood retaining more of the local-density signal while a coarser one averages it away. I experimented with fusing several $k$ scales to give the encoder fine and coarse summaries simultaneously; this helps on nested Thomas but not on Thomas, so the reported models use $k=5$ for Thomas and fused $k\in\{5,10,15\}$ for nested Thomas. That this precisely is a per-process choice is a weakness I return to in Section~\ref{sec:future}.

\paragraph{Encoder, head, and ablations.} For each choice of filtration I compute $H_0$ and $H_1$
persistence images. So on Thomas, which uses DTM$_5$ only, the model computes two diagrams per cloud, while Nested Thomas, which uses DTM$_{\{5,10,15\}}$ requires computing six diagrams (both homology dimensions for each of the three filtrations). All images are $64\times64$. Each is passed through a small convolutional stack: three $3\times3$ blocks with channel widths $(32,64,128)$, each followed by a ReLU and $2\times2$ max-pooling with spatial dropout ($p=0.2$) between blocks. Each block is a CoordConv layer \citep{liu2018coordconv}, concatenating two channels
holding each pixel's normalized coordinates before the convolution. I added this to the architecture because persistence image's axes are not arbitrary -- birth encodes local density and
persistence encodes separation -- so a pixel's absolute location, not just its
neighbourhood, is informative.  The feature map is reduced by adaptive average
pooling and projected to $E=64$, $\log N(\pc)$ is appended, and the result is
passed to an FCNN head with two hidden layers of width $64$ and $32$ (ReLU,
dropout $p=0.1$). 

When three scales are used they share one encoder and their
embeddings are concatenated. Every experiment uses AdamW ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$,
weight decay $3\times10^{-4}$), batch size $32$, up to $500$ epochs with
early-stopping patience $50$.

Each of these choices was ablated one axis at a time against this configuration
(Table~\ref{tab:branches}). Notable, when multiple filtration sare used, shared encoder beats three independent ones, which is desireable due to the significant different in trainable parameters. $H_0$
alone is competitive while $H_1$ alone collapses to chance, so the merge
structure carries most of the signal. Of the vectorizations,
Figure~\ref{fig:vectorization_schematic} shows the four compared on a common
diagram, and none of landscapes, silhouettes or Betti curves improved on
persistence images on either process. Two further arms isolate where the signal
lives: training on $\log N$ alone collapses to the chance level on both
processes, while removing $\log N$ from the full model costs little on nested
Thomas ($0.192\to0.203$) and is merely noisy on Thomas. The persistence images,
not the point count, are doing the work.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Estimation performance and baseline comparison}
\label{ssec:results}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

I compare ParamNet against the classical and neural baselines of
Section~\ref{sec:intro}: minimum contrast fit to Ripley's $K$ and, separately,
to the pair correlation function $g$, and \texttt{vihrs} -- my re-implementation
of the neural estimator of \citet{vihrs2022neural}: a 1-D CNN over the
edge-corrected, centred Ripley $L(r)-r$ curve plus $\log N(\pc)$, i.e.\ the
pipeline of Section~\ref{ssec:method} with the persistence-image feature
replaced by a single classical radial summary. All methods are run on the same
clouds and splits, which are made by parameter vector so that no parameter value
appears in both training and evaluation. Chance level is measured by retraining
the default model on randomly permuted labels.

\texttt{vihrs} beats ParamNet on Thomas: its per-parameter losses
($\mathcal{L}^\kappa=0.192$, $\mathcal{L}^\mu=0.122$,
$\mathcal{L}^\sigma=0.029$) are each individually lower than ParamNet's own
($0.201$, $0.134$, $0.040$; Table~\ref{tab:perparam}). So on this single-level
process, the topological pipeline does not beat a hand-built radial summary. On
nested Thomas, ParamNet gains an advantage on the density and count targets
while \texttt{vihrs} maintains an edge on both scale parameters. $L(r)-r$ is
built to resolve a characteristic radius directly, while a density-based
topological feature is not, and it is only on the more complex two-level process
that this trade-off favours the topological pipeline.

Both neural methods dominate minimum contrast on Thomas by roughly an order of
magnitude, but for two different reasons. On $K$ the estimator achieves losses
of $0.365$--$0.399$ for $6/10$ seeds -- close to \texttt{vihrs} -- and
$0.903$--$2.056$ on the remaining $4/10$, which is the near-Poisson flatness
described in Section~\ref{sec:intro}. On $g$ every seed is worse than chance,
indicating systematic bias rather than scattered non-convergence; the likely
cause is my contrast exponent $c=1$, set by analogy with
\citet{diggle2003spatial}'s $K$-based rule of thumb rather than tuned, where
$c=1/4$ is the value validated for aggregated patterns. I report the number but
do not read it as a statement about contrast estimation on $g$ in general.

Finally, parent intensity $\kappa$ is the hardest target and the cluster scale
$\sigma$ the easiest, for every method on both processes and by a wide margin.
This ordering holds regardless of estimator, so it looks like a property of the
estimation problem itself rather than an artifact of any one feature set or
architecture.

\section{Discussion, limitations and future work}
\label{sec:future}
% #############################################################################

The results support three claims. Persistence images are informative about
Neyman-Scott parameters: both processes sit roughly an order of magnitude below
the label-shuffle ceiling, and the two ``Features'' arms of
Table~\ref{tab:branches} place that signal in the images rather than in
$\log N$. They are not better than a well-chosen radial summary on a
single-level process: \texttt{vihrs} wins on all three Thomas parameters, and on
both scale parameters of nested Thomas. The advantage is confined to the
two-level process and to its density and count targets, which is what one would
expect of a feature built from local density rather than from a characteristic
radius. Two secondary observations: the ordering of parameter difficulty is
stable across feature sets and architectures, so it reads as a property of the
estimation problem; and the margin over minimum contrast is not a single effect,
being seed-to-seed instability on $K$ and a systematic bias traceable to an
untuned contrast exponent on $g$. Neither number is a verdict on contrast
estimation.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Multiple models.} The number of filtration scales is a
    per-process choice with no established rule beyond experimentation and
    comparison of validation losses, which undercuts the process-agnostic
    framing of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Always fusing would restore a single
    model at $3\times$ the persistence cost and $0.001$ on Thomas, i.e.\ inside
    seed noise; attention over scales might be the fix.
    \item \textbf{Competitiveness.} Three alternative vectorizations were
    implemented and none improved on persistence images. This is evidence about
    these summaries under this architecture and this design distribution, not a
    general claim about vectorizations.
    \item \textbf{No advantage on Thomas.} \texttt{vihrs} wins on every
    parameter of the single-level process, and on both scale parameters of the
    nested one.
    \item \textbf{The design distribution is a prior.} The trained model
    approximates a posterior summary under it, so its ranges must cover the
    intended application domain.
    \item \textbf{Computational cost.} Persistence computation scales badly in
    $n$; this is why the point budget of Section~\ref{ssec:simulator} is capped
    at 800. The inference cost per cloud is currently unmeasured.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Planned directions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
    \item The encoder architecture: aim to design a universal architecture that
    is process-agnostic.
    \item Inhomogeneous / non-stationary processes, where classical closed forms
    are least available and the topological approach has the most to gain. The
    comparator is the two-step procedure of \citet{waagepetersen2009twostep},
    which needs the covariates observed and the intensity correctly specified; a
    simulation-trained estimator needs neither.
    \item Apply to real data. Galaxy catalogues if the running example of
    Section~\ref{sec:intro} is cosmological. The Barro Colorado Island tree data
    of \citet{waagepetersen2009twostep} might be a second target: a small public
    data set already fitted with a Thomas process.
\end{itemize}

\part{Hessian Spectra During Grokking}

\section{Grokking and motivation}
\label{sec:hess-intro}
% #############################################################################

Grokking is a training dynamic in which a model attains near-perfect training
accuracy quickly, remains at chance-level test accuracy for an extended period,
and then transitions sharply to high test accuracy long after the training loss
has saturated. The delay is the object of interest: whatever separates the
memorizing phase from the generalizing one is not visible in the training loss,
which suggests the local geometry of the loss landscape rather than the loss
value as the informative quantity. This project takes the Hessian spectrum as
the description of that geometry, following \citet{fort2018goldilocks}, and
estimates it by the Lanczos-based approach of \citet{yao2020pyhessian}, which
recovers spectral information without forming the matrix. I implemented that
machinery directly rather than adopting the PyHessian library, so that the
tracked observables and their numerical treatment remained under my control.

The current experiments use modular addition ($a + b \bmod p$) with a small
decoder-only transformer trained with AdamW and weight decay large enough to
penalize the pure-memorization solution. Training accuracy reaches
$\sim\!100\%$ within a few thousand optimization steps, while test accuracy
remains near chance ($1/p$) for between hundreds and tens of thousands of
further steps before rising above $90\%$. The length of that delay, and its
dependence on weight decay and initialization scale, is the phenomenon this part
of the review addresses.

% #############################################################################
\section{Spectral observables}
\label{sec:hess-observables}
% #############################################################################

For parameters $\theta \in \R^n$ and loss $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$, the Hessian is
$\bH = \nabla^2_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta) \in \R^{n\times n}$, symmetric with
eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_n$. This is the true Hessian
rather than a Gauss--Newton or Fisher surrogate, so it may be indefinite:
negative eigenvalues correspond to directions of negative curvature, which a
positive semidefinite surrogate suppresses by construction. Rather than
individual eigenvalues, eight scalar summaries of the full spectrum are logged
per checkpoint: the top eigenvalue; a robust bulk/outlier threshold
(\texttt{bulk\_edge}) and the count of outliers above it; the trace; the
spectral entropy and its exponential (\texttt{effective\_rank}); the mass on
meaningfully negative eigenvalues; and a conditioning proxy given by the ratio
of top eigenvalue to bulk edge. Definitions and derivations are given in the
companion note \texttt{reports/spectral\_formalism.tex}.

% #############################################################################
\section{Matrix-free spectral estimation}
\label{sec:hess-lanczos}
% #############################################################################

For a network with $n$ in the $10^3$--$10^7$ range, forming $\bH$ explicitly is
infeasible, so the computational primitive is the Hessian-vector product.
Writing $g(\theta) = \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta)$, Pearlmutter's
identity gives $\bH v = \nabla_\theta(g(\theta)^\top v)$ from two backward
passes. Supplying $m$ such products to the Lanczos recurrence, initialized at a
random unit vector, yields an $m$-dimensional tridiagonal reduction of $\bH$
whose Ritz values and eigenvector weights form a moment-matching approximation
to the spectral measure of $\bH$ restricted to the corresponding Krylov
subspace. Averaging over $k$ independent random probe vectors --- stochastic
Lanczos quadrature --- gives a pooled estimate of the spectral measure, and
every observable of Section~\ref{sec:hess-observables} is a functional of that
pooled estimate. The Krylov depth $m$ and probe count $k$ are the two parameters
trading compute against accuracy.

% #############################################################################
\section{Estimator validation and $(m,k)$ convergence}
\label{sec:hess-validation}
% #############################################################################

All eight observables were validated against two exact-reference fixtures with
known ground-truth spectra: a small MLP whose Hessian is materialized in full,
and an engineered quadratic-form fixture with a production-like ratio between
the top eigenvalue and the bulk. The validation identified two estimator
defects, distinct from mere under-convergence. First, the original
\texttt{bulk\_edge} and \texttt{conditioning} definitions used an unweighted
median and MAD over the Ritz values, which was biased by 30--50\% at every
Krylov depth: Lanczos node placement is moment-matching rather than
density-matching, so an unweighted median over-trusts the sparse nodes near the
spectral edge. Second, the original \texttt{negative\_mass} definition
integrated mass immediately either side of the zero crossing and therefore
measured Lanczos noise rather than curvature; it is now margin-thresholded. Both
are corrected in the current code. Table~\ref{tab:hess-mk} reports the resulting
per-observable convergence requirements.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:hess-experiments}
% #############################################################################

Three datasets appear across the project's history. Two-spirals, a
two-dimensional binary classification problem, was the setting in which the
Hessian tooling was first built and tested, before a full architectural rewrite
of the codebase. MNIST and FashionMNIST followed, with an MLP, once the focus
moved to grokking dynamics proper; an FGSM adversarial-robustness check was run
alongside the curvature measurements then. Modular addition, with a
small decoder-only transformer on $a+b \bmod p$, is where all current
experiments run and is the source of every measured number reported below.

Two hyperparameters control the onset of grokking on this task, and both were
swept with logged runs (AdamW, effectively full-batch, $p=91$, $150{,}000$
steps). Weight decay was swept from $0.45$ to $1.0$ in steps of $0.05$, ten
seeds per setting. Initialization scale was swept at $\alpha \in \{1,8\}$, where
every parameter is multiplied by the scalar $\alpha$ after standard
initialization. The trends are monotone. At fixed $\alpha$, larger weight decay
produces an earlier generalization onset: the mean onset step falls from
$\sim\!3150$ at $wd=0.45$ to $\sim\!1350$ at $wd=1.0$, under a
first-crosses-twice-chance onset definition. At fixed $wd=1.0$, $\alpha=8$
roughly doubles the onset delay relative to $\alpha=1$ ($\sim\!2890$ against
$\sim\!1350$ steps). This sweep did not log Hessian spectra during training:
at this scale the cost of doing so alongside 240 training runs was prohibitive.
That omission is the gap addressed in Section~\ref{sec:hess-next}.

\section{Current results and their status}
\label{sec:hess-status}
% #############################################################################

The reproducible result currently supported by logged data is the sweep of
Section~\ref{sec:hess-experiments}: grokking onset on modular addition is
systematically and monotonically controlled by weight decay and initialization
scale. The remaining material in this section is either historical or not yet
run on real data, and is separated accordingly.

\paragraph{Recalled spectral collapse (unevidenced).} During the earlier
MNIST-era phase of the project I observed what appeared to be a spectral
collapse, with eigenvalues above roughly $1$ disappearing around the grokking
transition. This recollection is not currently supported by logged data, a
saved figure, or a write-up anywhere in the current repository: it predates the
architectural rewrite and was not retained. It is recorded here as an
observation to be re-established, not as a demonstrated result.

\paragraph{Gauss--Newton decomposition (not retained).} Before the same rewrite
I worked on the decomposition $\bH = \bG + \bF$, in which $\bG$ depends only on
the sensitivity of the network's outputs to its parameters and is positive
semidefinite, while $\bF$ carries the signed prediction residuals; $\bF$ is
therefore the sole source of negative curvature, and $\bH \to \bG$ as the fit
improves. On a small two-spirals MLP at initialization the decomposition held to
machine precision ($\lambda_{\min}(\bG) \approx -3\times10^{-17}$,
decomposition residual $\approx 2\times10^{-17}$), and the effective rank of
$\bG$ was $\approx 3$ against $337$ total parameters, indicating persistent
low-rank structure. This machinery was correct, but it predates the
architectural rewrite and was not retained; the current codebase tracks the true
Hessian only.

\paragraph{Weight-norm normalisation and the gate figure (implemented, not yet
run on real data).} Curvature scales as $\|\theta\|^{-2}$ under a uniform
rescaling of the parameters, so raw trace and top-eigenvalue traces move
whenever the weight norm does, independently of any change in the loss geometry.
A normalisation by weight norm is implemented for this reason, and a
corresponding two-panel diagnostic figure --- test accuracy and weight norm
above, weight-norm-corrected trace, top eigenvalue and effective rank below,
with the onset step marked --- is implemented end to end
(\texttt{analysis/transitions.py}, \texttt{analysis/make\_figures.py}) and
verified against synthetic input. Neither has been run against a spectral
parquet from an actual training run. This figure is the instrument that would
test the recalled collapse above.

\section{Next steps}
\label{sec:hess-next}
% #############################################################################

Returning to this project after a period away identified two gaps in the earlier
work: no systematic accuracy analysis of the $(m,k)$ approximation had been
performed, and no observable had been normalized by parameter count or weight
norm, despite the $\|\theta\|^{-2}$ scaling noted above. Both gaps are now
closed at the level of tested code (Sections~\ref{sec:hess-validation}
and~\ref{sec:hess-status}); neither has been applied to production data.

The immediate next step is mechanical. $9{,}465$ checkpoints from the
weight-decay sweep are already on disk; running
\texttt{scripts/spectral\_from\_checkpoints.py} over a subset of them would
produce the first spectral parquet the project has had from real training runs.
At that point the gate figure becomes a result rather than validated-but-unrun
code, and the recalled collapse of Section~\ref{sec:hess-status} can be tested
with error bars at $(m,k)$ settings whose accuracy is characterized.

Beyond that, \citet{tuci2026generalization} is the most promising direction to
build on: it derives a generalization bound from the full Hessian spectrum and
connects it explicitly to grokking, which is close to the question motivating
this work. The stochastic Hessian-trace estimators of
\citet{liu2023regularizing} were read alongside the Gauss--Newton literature
while the trace estimator of Section~\ref{sec:hess-lanczos} was being built.
