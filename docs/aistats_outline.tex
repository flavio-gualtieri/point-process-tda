\section*{Abstract (draft)}

Fitting a spatial point-process model to a single observed pattern is usually judged by whether the
fitted family and parameters match the truth. Near complete spatial randomness (CSR), where distinct
models generate patterns that cannot be told apart, this is the wrong target. We propose instead to
judge a fit by whether it \emph{reproduces the pattern}: patterns simulated from the fit are compared
with an independent replicate by a strictly proper kernel score on local configurations, normalised
between the true model and CSR. Under this criterion we study amortized model selection and parameter
inference over eight families (cluster, Cox, hard-core and lattice-like processes). A single
classifier whose Poisson probability serves as the CSR filter is followed by one parameter estimator
per family, which is exactly how the joint posterior over family and parameters factorises. We find
three things.
(i)~Near CSR, identifiability is a property of the data rather than the model. Eleven classifiers,
spanning gradient-boosted trees, linear models and neural networks on summary functions and
persistent-homology features, reach the same accuracy (the best four within $0.002$) and lose detection
as one physical coordinate approaches its CSR limit. At that limit the proper score cannot distinguish
the true model from CSR either.
(ii)~Family accuracy understates fidelity. The pipeline names the generating family for $53\%$ of
patterns, yet reproduces held-out replicates with a skill of $0.93$. Among strongly structured
patterns, the third assigned to the wrong family are reproduced as faithfully (skill $0.95$) as the
rest ($0.94$).
(iii)~Topological features never make parameter estimates worse, and help where second-order
statistics are blind by construction: they cut parameter error by $15$--$18\%$ for a process whose
$K$-function equals Poisson's.
We also report two negative results. An optimal-transport score between whole patterns lacks the
power to tell the true model from CSR. Restricting training to the identifiable regime lowers error
within that regime, but the gain is lost once a pattern's regime has to be inferred from the pattern
itself.

\section*{The one-line pitch}

Judged by label accuracy, amortized point-process fitting looks weak ($0.53$ on 8 families). Judged by
whether the fit reproduces the pattern, it is strong ($0.93$). The gap is not a failure of the method. It is
a measurable identifiability limit near CSR, and the paper's job is to show it is a property of the data.

\textbf{What we do not lead with.} ``A realization engine'' (reads as a tool paper), and persistent homology
(its gains are real but small; it is a supporting result, not the headline).

\section*{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Task and criterion.} Single-pattern model fitting cast as predictive fidelity. A strictly
    proper kernel score on radius-$R$ local configurations is anchored at the true model (regret) and at CSR
    (gain), giving skill $=1-\sum\text{regret}/\sum\text{gain}$. Fits are scored on an \emph{independent}
    replicate, so a fit cannot be flattered by the pattern it was estimated from. A power check shows the obvious
    alternative (a Wasserstein-1 energy score) fails.
  \item \textbf{The CSR wall.} Across 8 families and 11 classifiers / 10 estimators, detection, classification
    and parameter error collapse at the same boundary, described by one physical coordinate per family (cluster
    overlap $\omega$, signal-to-noise $\zeta$) times a power of $\nb$. The proper score confirms the boundary
    independently of any learned model: there, even the true model does not beat CSR.
  \item \textbf{A pipeline that is the posterior's factorisation.} $p(f,\theta\mid x)=p(f\mid x)\,p(\theta\mid x,f)$:
    one classifier (its $p(\text{poisson}\mid x)$ \emph{is} the CSR filter), then per-family estimators. The flat
    classifier beats a hierarchical cascade (group, then family, with a reject class) by $5.4$ points.
  \item \textbf{Where topology helps.} Adding PH to classical summaries never makes estimates significantly
    worse and helps most where second-order statistics cannot see the structure (cell process: $K(r)=\pi r^2$
    exactly; ring process: loops in $H_1$).
  \item \textbf{Negative results, reported.} Wasserstein scoring has no power; training-set filtering by regime
    helps in regime but cannot be exploited when the regime must be inferred.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Before submission}

\textbf{Must have.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Classical baseline: \texttt{spatstat::kppm} (minimum contrast and Palm likelihood) for Thomas, Mat\'ern cluster and
    LGCP, scored with the same end-to-end score. The other five families have no closed-form fit, which is itself the
    argument for amortized inference.
  \item One or two real patterns (candidates in the bank's $n$ range: \texttt{redwoodfull}, \texttt{finpines},
    \texttt{spruces}), rescaled to the unit window, scored without an oracle (fit vs CSR vs \texttt{kppm}).
  \item Fig.~1 and the per-model regime curves.
  \item Confirm the AISTATS 2027 deadline; historically it falls in early October.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Cheap and worth it.} Soft routing (simulate from the family mixture weighted by $p(f\mid x)$, using existing
posteriors); a calibration check of $p(f\mid x)$.

\textbf{Future work, framed as the upgrade the factorisation allows.} Posterior rather than point estimates for $\theta$
(distributional trees or neural posterior estimation) and posterior-predictive sampling; learned permutation-invariant
summaries of the raw points (DeepSets / GNN) as the stronger learned baseline.

\textbf{Anticipated reviewer questions.} Why not a classical fit? (\S5 baseline.) Why only synthetic data? (real-data demo.)
Why point estimates? (limitations; soft routing partly answers it.) Is PH just another feature? (\S6: it helps exactly where
second-order summaries are blind, and never hurts.) Why is accuracy so low? (\S4: the CSR wall; lead with it, do not hide it.)
